This article is about the testing stack behind Poker Champ, a real-time Texas Hold'em platform where correctness means money conservation, seat churn survival, and hands that keep completing under reconnect chaos.

You will get why happy-path tests fail here, the layers I ended up needing (invariants, soaks, headless churn, Playwright), and the timing bugs that made the suite necessary.

Happy-path tests are not enough

A fold that works once in a unit test is worthless if the hand deadlocks on the twentieth reconnect. Poker state is a moving machine: streets, timers, seats, pots, side pots, showdowns, auto-actions for disconnected players. Money has to conserve. Turns have to stay owned by someone eligible. Snapshots have to keep advancing. If any of that stalls, the table feels broken even when the rules code is "correct."

So the standard I ended up enforcing was not "does this scenario pass." It was closer to: can this engine survive randomized legal play, seat churn, and long runs without losing chips or freezing the street.

Layers on layers

The repo grew into a stack of confidence gates instead of one test folder.

At the bottom: betting rules, side pots, showdown determinism, ledger accounting, fold-path equivalence, money-safety suites. Fast, specific, mean when they fail.

Above that: fuzz and random-walk soaks against the dealer itself. Dozens of hands, legal actions only, disconnect and reconnect injected on purpose, chip-mass checks on every settlement. These are not "long tests for coverage." They hunt deadlocks, livelocks, stale queued auto-actions, and payout drift.

Then room-level multi-table soaks: concurrent PokerRoom instances, humans and bots, hand-completion rates and rejection budgets. Nightly profiles push harder. A validation script can spin multiple tables and fail the run if hands stop completing cleanly.

Then headless churn harnesses against a live local server. Join, leave, thrash seats, fold storms, all-in ladders. Strict mode fails fast. Collect mode keeps going and aggregates findings. On failure it writes repro artifacts: event timelines, diagnostics, the violation that ended the run. That alone changed how fast I could debug intermittent hangs.

Then Playwright. Two-browser stack consistency. Seat churn. Rejoin and buy-in edge cases. Lessons runtime. Tournaments busting out. The UI had to agree with the engine, not just look fine in one happy session.

And still more: bot brain heatmaps, tournament milestone integration suites stretching across abandon, rebuy, late reg, orphan tables, payouts, and freezeout endings. Client unit tests for stores, realtime guards, action bars, money adapters. A verify script that chains SDK checks, core server tests, soak, headless harness, knip, dependency graphs, and more before you pretend you are done.

What the insane bugs actually looked like

The failures that taught me the most were timing bugs, not rule typos.

A bot auto-action queued on one street could get discarded during an automatic street transition, then never re-driven. The table stalled on the flop with nobody acting. Intermittent. Seed-dependent. Invisible to a tidy unit test that never raced the queue against AUTO_TRANSITION.

Ghost tables were another gift. Rooms configured not to auto-dispose so players could reconnect. Fine, until a human left and bots kept a room "alive" forever in a half-started street. Stall monitors screamed. Nobody was really playing. The soak and observability work made those rooms impossible to ignore.

Rejoin grace periods, sitting out until next hand, mid-hand joiners who must wait for the next deal, abandoned seats, cash-out idempotency: every one of those is a tiny state machine living inside a bigger one. Miss an invariant and you do not get a polite error. You get a quiet table and angry money.

Why it had to be this heavy

Because poker is adversarial toward incomplete testing. Players invent the chaos for free. They refresh. They open two tabs. They leave on their turn. They come back after the auto-fold. They join a tournament late. They expect the pot to be fair anyway.

So I stopped treating tests as a chore at the end of a feature and started treating them as the product surface that proves the table can survive contact with humans. Unit tests protect the rules. Soaks protect time. Churn harnesses protect seats. Money invariants protect trust. E2E protects the story the player sees.

If that sounds like overkill for a side project, good. Real-time multiplayer with chips is not a todo app. The insane part was not writing a lot of tests. The insane part was realizing how many different kinds of tests you need before a poker hand can be boring again.
