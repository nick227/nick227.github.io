At Digital Harbor, my assignment was to help create the next generation of forms. Our team built about half a dozen prototypes, then turned the strongest ideas into reusable templates for customers.

Design for focus

We tried to keep one question in the user's eyeline at a time, with rarely more than two elements competing for attention: the input and a supporting visual. Strong hierarchy and readability came first. Users also needed to know where they were in the process, what remained, and whether they could review a summary before submitting.

Some prototypes used smooth snap scrolling. It can make scrolling feel less predictable, but the experiment forced each screen to have a clear purpose and exposed weak hierarchy quickly.

Make inputs smarter

Validation, rules, and input masks formed the baseline. We added autocomplete, automatic dropdowns, useful defaults, and suggestion chips to reduce errors and typing. We came to think of a smart input as predictive, error-resistant, and aware of enough context to help without restricting the user.

Inputs could also connect to API requests and trigger events elsewhere on the page. An answer might update the next question, prefill related information, change a visual, or display a calculated average across respondents. This made the form responsive during the process instead of waiting until after submission to provide value.

Reduce the work required

The interfaces people preferred generally required fewer clicks and less typing. In 2026, speech-to-text and AI assistance are increasingly useful for textareas that ask for longer explanations. Voice can capture an initial response, while AI can help organize it into a draft the user reviews and edits. The textarea remains available when precise writing matters.

Importing can reduce effort even further. If information already exists in a document, account, device, or another system, a form can parse and prefill what it recognizes, mark uncertainty, and ask the user to review the result. This is often more efficient than entering the same information field by field.

Our prototypes suggested that progress comes from asking less of the user: fewer questions, earlier validation, relevant predictions, and immediate feedback. The form still collects structured information, but the experience becomes easier to understand and faster to complete.
