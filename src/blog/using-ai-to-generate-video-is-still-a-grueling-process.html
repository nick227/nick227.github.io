This article is about making AI video in practice: demos look finished, but usable projects still mean many failed shots, consistency problems, model costs, and an edit that has to hold together.

You will get why I treat models as shot generators rather than full production systems, how pricing compares in real use, and what still makes the process grueling.

The prices are not as comparable as they look

Video models charge by the clip or by the generated second. Resolution, audio, model tier, and output length all affect the total, so there is no perfect one-line comparison. Using fal.ai as a common provider, this was the pricing I found in July 2026:

  • MiniMax Hailuo 2.3 Standard: about $0.28 for a six-second 768p generation. Hailuo Pro is listed around $0.49 per generation.
  • LTX 2.3: roughly $0.06–$0.08 per second at 1080p, or about $0.36–$0.48 for six seconds.
  • Veo 3.1 Fast: $0.10 per second without audio and $0.15 with audio at 720p or 1080p. Six seconds costs about $0.60 or $0.90.
  • Veo 3.1 Standard: $0.20 per second without audio and $0.40 with audio at 720p or 1080p. Six seconds costs about $1.20 or $2.40.

These prices will change, and the outputs are not equivalent. A cheap clip that cannot be used is more expensive than a higher-priced clip that makes the final edit. The cost I care about is not the price per generated second. It is the price per usable second.

LTX has not been usable for me

LTX is attractive on paper. It is open, fast, and inexpensive enough for experimentation. In my own tests, the results have not been usable. The clips may demonstrate motion, but they have not given me the visual quality or consistency I need to keep them in a finished project.

That does not mean LTX has no value. It may work for rough previs, local experiments, or workflows that can tolerate obvious artifacts. It simply has not cleared my minimum bar for final footage.

MiniMax can get the job done

MiniMax has been a practical middle option. It is affordable enough to try several versions of a shot, and I can get clips that survive the edit. It does not consistently give me the detail or interpretation I get from Veo, but it can turn a clear prompt or source image into workable motion.

This matters because most prompts need more than one attempt. The subject may move correctly while the camera does not. The first half may be useful while the last two seconds collapse. A background object may begin doing something nobody requested. MiniMax gives me enough room in the budget to rerun those shots.

Veo feels like a different generation

Moving from MiniMax to Veo is a night-and-day quality change in my experience. Veo adds detail, secondary motion, environmental behavior, and more imagination to the scene. It often understands that an animation needs more than moving the central subject from one position to another.

The output feels more directed. Lighting changes across the shot, backgrounds remain active, and camera movement has more purpose. Veo is also considerably more expensive, especially with audio, so I do not want to use the highest tier while I am still deciding what the shot should be.

My preferred workflow is to develop the composition with still images and lower-cost generations, then spend Veo money once the shot has a clear job in the edit.

fal.ai makes comparison much easier

I use fal.ai because it puts a large number of video models behind one account and a similar API pattern. I can compare MiniMax, Veo, LTX, Kling, Wan, Seedance, and other models without building a new integration and billing relationship for every test.

fal.ai charges according to each model's output unit, usually per second or per video. Failed server requests and time waiting in the queue are not billed. More importantly for development, switching models is often an endpoint change rather than a new application architecture.

The wide selection can also become its own problem. Testing every new model consumes the same budget and attention I am trying to save. I now want a reason to try a model: lower iteration cost, better character consistency, stronger audio, or a quality improvement large enough to change the final result.

Hedra is my choice for lip syncing

Full scene generation and character performance are different problems. When I already have a character image and need that character to perform audio, I am a big fan of Hedra.

Hedra has worked well for lip syncing speech and even music. Keeping that as a specialized step gives me more control than asking a general video model to invent the character, scene, performance, and audio synchronization in one attempt.

I still need to try Higgsfield and Seedance

I have not tested Higgsfield or Seedance enough to give either a fair review. Seedance may end up being one of the strongest options based on the examples and controls I have seen, but it has not passed through my own workflow yet.

That distinction matters. A model can look exceptional in a selected demo and still be frustrating when asked to produce a specific list of shots on a real budget.

The hard part begins after generation

Most first attempts at AI video fail because the tools are presented as if generation is the production. It is not. The production still needs shot planning, prompt development, continuity, selection, timing, sound, and editing.

A five-second clip may require five attempts. A one-minute video may need twenty planned shots and many more generations. The successful clips still need to be trimmed, sequenced, color matched, and supported by audio. Model quality has improved faster than this workflow has disappeared.

For now, simple visual styles remain the most reliable approach for me. Line drawings and limited animation ask the model to maintain fewer details across frames. Small inconsistencies look less destructive, and separate clips are easier to combine into one visual language.

Veo proves that AI video can produce genuinely impressive footage. It does not prove that making a good video has become easy. The gap between a generated clip and a finished piece is still where most of the work lives.
