The writing is on the wall. Every interface is drifting toward an AI chat. Support, search, dashboards, checkout flows, creative tools. If it has a blank box and a send button, someone is already calling it the future.

That creates the major problem with AI: typing.

Users do not want to type. A high percentage simply will not. They will bounce, tap something else, or sit there waiting for the product to guess. We keep building systems that assume the user will carefully explain what they need. Most people will not do that.

So the real job stops being "accept a prompt" and starts being "predict what they want to click."

The hat store problem

This is not as hard as it sounds if you are an online hat store. The user wants to buy a hat. Show them some hats. Let them click.

That is the joke, and also the point. After a full lap through chat UIs, copilots, and prompt engineering, we land back on information architecture. Layouts. Affordances. Obvious next actions. Pictures of the thing. Big buttons that mean something.

The future of AI UX may look less like a blank textarea and more like a well-designed storefront that happens to have a model behind it. Predict intent. Offer the answer as something clickable. Let the chat be the escape hatch, not the front door.

Text is a draining UX

Taking text is expensive for the user, whether the field is labeled chat or search. Keyboard input is friction. Every empty box asks them to invent language, remember vocabulary, and trust that the system will understand. That is a lot of work before they get anything back.

We lose people there. Not because the model is weak, but because the interface demanded labor up front.

If you cannot show a picture of what they want, or a set of big clear actions, then at least give them suggestion chips. Autocomplete the intent. Put the likely next sentence in front of their thumb. Make the first move for them.

In 2026, every input needs a microphone

There is no excuse anymore. Chrome already has built-in speech to text. Whisper is open source. Voice should be a default affordance on any field that currently asks someone to type a paragraph.

Designers keep wanting to be first. Users are about to start expecting AI to feel invisible: the right options appear, the mic works, the chips are already close, and the product does not make them perform for it.

Am I saying get rid of the textarea? Of course not. Keep it. Supercharge it. Let people who want precision still write. Let everyone else click, talk, or tap a suggestion that gets them 80% of the way there.

Who is helping with the massive prompt?

In design we are always looking ahead. The next problem is already here: the prompts are getting huge. Context, constraints, style rules, examples, edge cases. Nobody at the big labs seems to be seriously helping users build those prompts before the conversation starts.

The workaround people already use is absurd and revealing. Ask the LLM to write the prompt, then feed that prompt back into the same LLM. The model becomes the author of its own instructions because the product never bothered to meet the user halfway.

That should embarrass us a little. If the best way to use your interface is to leave it, write a better prompt somewhere else, and come back, then the interface is not doing its job.

What does an LLM look like without user prompts?

That is the question this whole proposal collapses into.

Maybe it looks like a hat store. Rows of likely intents. Images. Chips. Voice. Soft defaults. A textarea that is ready when you need it, quiet when you do not. An experience that predicts the click, absorbs the ambiguity, and only asks for language when language is actually the point.

Chat is coming for every surface. Fine. Just do not make typing the price of admission.
